Posterior Urethral Valves (PUV) in Children

A concise overview of posterior urethral valves, with emphasis on bladder dysfunction after relief of obstruction, long-term follow-up, and what remains uncertain.

Educational overview. This page does not provide medical advice for an individual child.

Overview

Posterior urethral valves (PUV) are a congenital cause of lower urinary tract obstruction in boys. The obstruction is in the posterior urethra, and it can affect the bladder, the upper urinary tracts, and renal function.

Effects on the bladder and on the kidneys are related but not identical, and they do not necessarily progress in parallel. This page is an overview. It is not a treatment guideline.

Initial assessment and diagnosis

Assessment brings together several sources of information: bladder and upper-tract ultrasound findings, renal function, and voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) when PUV is suspected. No single finding is interpreted in isolation, and this page does not give diagnostic thresholds.

Relief of obstruction

Initial bladder drainage and clinical stabilisation may be required. Endoscopic valve ablation is the standard definitive approach to relieve the obstruction when it is feasible. Technique, timing and any alternative approach depend on the child and are not covered here.

Bladder dysfunction after valve ablation

Relief of the anatomical obstruction does not necessarily normalise bladder function. Bladder dysfunction can persist or evolve over childhood and adolescence, and its pattern can change with age. It is not a single uniform picture, and it should not be reduced to one simple “valve bladder” phenotype.

This is why bladder function sits at the centre of long-term care and of the research focus described below. It links directly to three goals:

  • Pressure management: assessment of bladder storage and voiding pressures, because elevated bladder pressures can contribute to upper urinary tract deterioration
  • Continence: daytime and night-time control and voiding symptoms, which matter for quality of life as well as for the bladder itself
  • Upper-tract preservation: protecting the kidneys and ureters from the effects of an abnormal bladder

Long-term follow-up

Renal function and bladder function require long-term, in many patients lifelong, monitoring. Care continues beyond childhood, so transition into adult urology and nephrology care is part of planning. This page does not give follow-up intervals, which are individualised.

What clinicians follow

Renal function
How well the kidneys are working, and how this changes over time.
Upper-tract dilation
Whether the kidneys and ureters are dilated, and whether this is changing.
Bladder function
How the bladder stores and empties urine, including whether this is changing as the child grows.
Continence and voiding symptoms
Daytime and night-time continence and the child’s voiding pattern.
Urinary tract infection history
Whether infections have occurred, and how often.
Interval changes over time
Trends across visits and across childhood, rather than a single measurement.

Research focus

Posterior urethral valves and associated bladder dysfunction, with attention to pressure management, continence, and upper-tract preservation.

See Research for the full list of focus areas. These are areas of study and questions under investigation, not a list of findings; indexed publications are on the Publications page.

What remains uncertain

  • which children will develop progressive bladder dysfunction
  • how best to identify clinically important high-pressure bladder states
  • how bladder phenotype changes with age
  • which markers best predict upper-tract or renal deterioration
  • how to individualise bladder surveillance and intervention
  • which children ultimately require additional bladder-directed treatment
